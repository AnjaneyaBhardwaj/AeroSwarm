How Aeroptimize works

Three AI agents, one wing, zero screwdrivers.

Aeroptimize is a small team of AI characters that redesigns the cross-section of a race-car wing until it pushes down by exactly the right amount. They plan, sketch and nitpick. Physics calculators and a strict rulebook do the hands-on work, so every number you see is measured, never made up.

The job: an upside-down wing

An airplane wing lifts the plane up. Turn it upside down and it pushes a car down instead. Racers call that push downforce. It presses the tyres into the track, so the car can take corners faster without sliding.

There's a catch. A wing that pushes harder also drags more, like a small parachute that slows the car on the straights. Ask for too much and the air gives up and peels off the wing. That's a stall, and the grip disappears all at once.

The brief

  • Push exactly this hard: Cl −1.83, give or take 0.03
  • Keep drag under Cd 0.025
  • No stalling, with some margin to spare
  • Legal under the Formula Student rules
  • Confirmed by the slow, strict calculator

These numbers are for the slice, tested in air at 15 m/s (54 km/h) on a slice 300 mm long, front to back.

What exactly do they design?

Just the wing's cross-section, called an airfoil: the shape you'd see if you sliced the wing like a loaf of bread. It's flat and 2D: no wingspan, no end plates, and not the rest of the car.

That slice decides most of how a wing behaves, and it's what fast physics calculators can test in seconds. The full 3D wing is a later step.

Meet the crew

Three AI agents, each with one job. They talk through short typed forms, the way a pit crew uses hand signals, and none of them is allowed to touch the wing directly.

More curve, less angle. Trust me.

The Chief

Chief aerodynamicist · the boss

Reads every result so far, spots the pattern and calls the next move: which of the wing's knobs to turn, and which way. When a design looks promising, the Chief sends it to the slow, strict calculator for an official check.

Quirk: has never held a screwdriver and sees no reason to start.

Camber 7.62%, angle 7.00°. I've attached a paragraph on why.

The CAD Engineer

Shape designer · the precise one

Turns the Chief's big ideas into exact numbers for the four knobs: how curved the wing is, where the curve peaks, how thick it is and how steeply it meets the air. May overrule the Chief, but only with a written reason.

Quirk: explains the physics of every change, whether or not anyone asked.

Hmm. Is that air peeling off the back?

The Critic

Inspector · the sceptic

Studies each test result for trouble: air peeling away, a wing on the edge of stalling, sneaky extra drag. The Critic can mark a result down, never up.

Quirk: has never said “perfect” without checking twice.

The machines not agents

They calculate and check. They never make decisions.

The Hare NeuralFoil

A small neural network trained on the Tortoise's answers. It guesses how a wing will behave in a blink: great for trying lots of ideas, not trusted with the final word.

The Tortoise XFoil

A classic physics program: slow, careful and strict. Only the Tortoise can make a design official.

The Referee plain code

Checks every proposed shape against the rulebook and the Chief's limits, and shows a red card to anything illegal or already tried, before it's tested.

House rules

  • Agents fill in forms. They never write code or touch the wing.

  • Numbers come from physics. Every number is measured by a calculator or the rulebook check, never typed by an agent.

  • Red cards first. Illegal or repeated shapes are bounced before anyone tests them.

  • No grading your own homework. The Critic can only mark results down.

Pocket dictionary

Airfoil
The shape of a wing's cross-section: the slice the crew designs.
Downforce
The push that presses a car into the track. More push, more grip in corners.
Drag
Air resistance: the parachute effect that costs speed on the straights.
Stall
When the air stops following the wing and peels off. The grip goes suddenly.
Camber
How curved the wing is. More curve gives more push and more drag.
Angle of attack
How steeply the wing meets the air. Steeper pushes harder, until it stalls.
Chord
The slice's length from its front edge to its back edge. Here 300 mm.
Cl and Cd
The slice's push and drag numbers, scaled so wings of any size compare fairly. Cl is negative here because the push points down.
